What is Steering?
7
Let’s imagine a small creature in a game:  like a frog, a bird, or a car.
We want it to move in a realistic and intelligent way:
▪The frog should move toward the fly.
▪The bird should turn smoothly, not instantly.
▪The car should avoid hitting obstacles and follow the road.
All these actions are examples of steering:             steering means deciding how to 
change direction and speed smoothly over time (steer: guide, direct, pilot, lead..)
Steering behavior is a rule that tells a game character how to move in a way that 
looks natural and intelligent, based on what it sees or wants to do.  
What is Steering?
Real-life Example:
Think of driving a car or riding a bike:
▪
You don’t instantly face a new direction:  you turn the 
steering wheel gradually.
▪
You don’t stop instantly: you press the brakes and slow 
down.
▪
You don’t drive straight into a wall: you steer away.
So Games use the same idea: 
▪
Characters don’t jump from one place to another.
▪
They move, turn, and slow down using small steering 
corrections every frame.
Why We Use Steering in Games
Steering is used whenever we want believable motion:

How Movement Happens?
Imagine driving a car:
▪
Position: where your car is right now on the road.
▪
Velocity: how fast and in what direction we’re driving/the speed of something given a direction!
▪
Acceleration: how your speed changes: pressing gas, braking, or turning the wheel. 
Now in games, we update these things many times every second => frames per second or FPS.
➢Acceleration changes velocity.
➢Velocity changes position.
▪
FPS: how often the game updates each second. 
▪
Δ time (delta time): the amount of time that passes between two frames(two updates). 
▪
Δt  is the inverse of FPS => Δt= 1/ FPS
The Tiny Physics Behind Steering
The Formulas:
For each frame, we do:
velocity = velocity + acceleration * dt
position = position + velocity * dt
Remember: dt means “delta time”, how many seconds have passed since the last frame (usually around 
1/60 of a second).
The Tiny Physics Behind Steering
Now we ask: what makes acceleration happen?
In real life, forces change our velocity:
▪
Gas pedal pushes the car forward.
▪
Brakes apply force backward.
▪
Turning the wheel changes the direction of the force.
In our game, steering behaviors are those forces!
▪
Each steering behavior computes a steering force: a small vector that says “turn this way”
or “slow down a bit”.
▪
Then we convert that force into acceleration: 
acceleration = steering_force / mass, if we treat mass = 1 (to keep things simple), then:
acceleration = steering_force

Limiting Speed and Force
To make motion realistic, we usually limit how strong or fast things can go.
▪
max_speed => how fast the agent can move
▪
max_force => how sharp it can turn
We use these limits so the agent can’t instantly spin or move at light speed.

▪
The force needed to change current velocity toward the desired direction.
▪
steering = desired_velocity - current_velocity

▪
acceleration = steering_force / mass
▪
If we assume mass = 1 (common in simple games): acceleration = steering_force

Steering Behaviors Overview
▪Steering Behaviors are designed to give autonomous characters the ability to  
navigate around their world in a life-like and improvisational manner.
Steering Behaviors Overview
▪They are not based on complex strategies  involving path planning or global  
calculations but instead use local  information. 
What is it:
▪
Seek makes the character move toward a target position: it tries to match its position with 
the target’s position.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used when we want an agent/ to go to a specific point. For example, the frog moving 
toward a clicked location or a missile homing in on a target.
How It Works:
▪
Find the direction from the agent to the target.
▪
Turn that direction into a desired velocity : “this is how I want to move.”
▪
Compute the steering force = desired velocity - current velocity.
▪
Apply that steering force to slowly turn and move toward the target.
Seek Behavior
Seek Behavior
▪
The green triangle = the agent (character).
▪
The circle with a cross = the target.
▪
The green arrow = current velocity (where it’s moving 
now).
▪
The black arrow = desired velocity (direction of the 
target).
▪
The blue arrow = seek steering (the correction to face 
the target).
▪
The curved path = how the agent turns smoothly to 
reach the target.
What is it:
▪
Flee is the opposite of Seek: it makes the character move away from a threat or target
Goal/Use:
▪
Used when an agent must escape danger, like a fly running away from a snake.
▪
How It Works:
▪
Find the direction away from the target (reverse of Seek).
▪
Set that as the desired velocity.
▪
The steering force turns the agent away from the threat.

Flee Behavior
What is it:
▪
Arrival is a gentler version of Seek. It makes the agent slow down as it gets close to the target.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used when you want your character to stop smoothly, like a car braking before a stop sign or 
the frog slowing down near a fly.
▪
How It Works:
▪
Compute the desired velocity like Seek.
▪
If the target is far away, move at full speed.
▪
If it’s close, reduce speed proportionally to distance.
▪
The closer you are, the slower you move: until you stop exactly at the target.

What is it:
▪
Pursue: Chase a moving target by predicting where it will be, not just where it is.
▪
Evade: Escape from a moving pursuer by predicting where it will reach you and avoiding that 
point.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used when both the hunter and the prey are moving, like a snake chasing a frog or a frog 
dodging an incoming snake.
▪
How It Works:
▪
Measure the target’s velocity (how fast it’s moving).
▪
Predict its future position after a short time. 
▪
Seek (for Pursue) or Flee (for Evade) that future position.

What is it:
▪
Wander gives the agent a natural-looking random movement, it changes direction gradually, 
not instantly.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used to make background characters or animals move in a relaxed, natural, and unpredictable 
way.(Example: flies buzzing randomly.)
▪
How It Works:
▪
Place a circle ahead of the agent in the direction it’s moving.
▪
Pick a random point on that circle as the new temporary target.
▪
Seek that point each frame.
▪
Move the point slightly each frame so the direction drifts smoothly.

What is it:
▪
Path Following keeps the agent close to a predefined path or road.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used for objects that must follow a trail, river, or patrol route without leaving it.
▪
How It Works:
▪
Predict where the agent will be soon (look ahead).
▪
Find the closest point on the path to that future position.
▪
Set a target point slightly ahead on the path.
▪
Seek that target to stay near the path center.

▪

Goal/Use:

Goal/Use:

Used for navigating spaces with static objects.

What is it:
▪
Keep a comfortable distance from nearby agents, avoid crowding or overlapping.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used in flocks, crowds, or swarms to give each character personal space.
▪
How It Works:
▪
For each nearby neighbor, calculate a small force that pushes the agent away.
▪
Add all these forces together to get the total separation force.
▪
Stronger push = closer neighbor.

▪
Alignment makes agents face the same direction as their neighbors.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used so groups of agents travel smoothly in the same general direction (part of flocking).

How It Works:

What is it:
▪
Flocking combines Separation + Cohesion + Alignment so agents move together as a natural, 
flowing group.
▪
Leader Following adds one more goal, stay behind and follow the leader.
Goal/Use:
▪
Used for birds flying together, fish schooling, or followers moving behind a leader (like a 
squad).
▪
How It Works:
▪
Separation: don’t bump into neighbors.
▪
Cohesion: stay with the group.
▪
Alignment: match the group’s direction.
▪
Leader Following: seek a point behind the leader while avoiding it.
Flocking / Leader Following
Flocking / Leader Following
▪The green triangles = group members.

Note that:
The flocking behavior relies on blending three simple steering  behaviors:  
▪
Separation: move away from characters that are too close
▪
Alignment: move in the same direction and at the same velocity as the  group
▪
Cohesion: move toward the center of mass of the flock
In flocking behavior, separation is  more important than cohesion,  which is more important than  
alignment. 
Flocking Behavior
▪
Individually, steering behaviors can achieve a good degree of  movement sophistication. 
However, by combining steering  behaviors together, even more complex movement can be  
achieved. 
There are two methods of combining steering behaviors:  blending and arbitration.
▪
Blending combines behaviors by executing all the steering behaviors  and combining their 
results using a set of weights.
▪
Arbitration selects one or more steering behaviors to have complete  control over the 
character.
