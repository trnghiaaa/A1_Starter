Maths / Physics Preliminaries
For this course, you should familiarise yourself with the following:
•
What a vector is (a quantity that has both magnitude/length and direction).
•
The length of vector v is written as |v|.
•
A unit vector is a vector of length 1. Typically written as û.
•
To get a unit vector in the direction of v, divide by its length, i.e., û = v / |v|.
•
Velocity: v = dx/dt, where x = position and t = time.*
•
For an object moving at constant speed, v = ∆x / ∆t.
•
Acceleration: a = dv/dt.
•
Under a constant force, a = ∆v / ∆t.
•
Newton’s second law: F = ma.
•
See the Khan Academy links on the Week 2 Canvas page if you need to brush up.
*Don’t stress, we’re not going to get into derivatives. Just think of velocity as “how quickly the position is changing”.
6
Example (in 1D)
An object of mass 2kg is currently travelling at a velocity of -5m/s in the x-direction.
(Negative velocity = moving left).
A constant force is applied to the object, increasing its velocity to +10m/s after 3 seconds.
What is the magnitude of the force?
∆v = 10 – (-5) = 15m/s
a = ∆v / ∆t = 15 / 3 = 5m/s2
F = ma = 2 * 5 = 10
Force is measured in “Newtons” (N), so you will see this written as F = 10N.

Steering Behaviours: General Approach
Each character has a desired velocity, i.e., the velocity they would like to be moving at.
•
A shark chasing a fish would like to be moving towards the fish at its maximum speed.*
•
The fish would like to be moving away from the shark at its maximum speed.
Key idea:
Rather than immediately setting velocity = desiredVelocity, gradually accelerate to desiredVelocity:
∆v = desiredVelocity - currentVelocity
a = ∆v / accelerationTime
F = ma
   = m * (desiredVelocity - currentVelocity) / accelerationTime
*An intelligent shark might do something else, as we’ll discuss soon!

Steering Behaviours: General Approach
11
Seek
The seek steering behaviour can be paraphrased as:
“I’d like to be moving towards my target position at my maximum speed”.
Let v = targetPos - currentPos (i.e., a vector from the current position to the target).
Let û denote a unit vector (vector of length 1) in the direction of v.
The desired velocity is then:
desiredVelocity = û * maxSpeed
In Unity, this is:
desiredVelocity = (targetPos - currentPos).normalized * maxSpeed;
12
Flee
“I’d like to be moving away from the target at my maximum speed”.
The calculation of the desired velocity is identical to seek, except that the resultant vector is 
flipped to point away from the target.

Arrive
Idea: Modify seek to slow down once within a certain distance of the target to avoid overshooting.
•
Let d be the distance of the character from the target.
•
Let r be the radius of a circle centred on the target.
•
If d >= r, don’t adjust the desired velocity (just use seek).
•
If d < r, scale seek’s desired velocity by d/r.
•
As the character approaches the target, the desired velocity approaches zero.
15
Pursuit and Evasion
•
Pursuit is similar to seek except that the target is another moving character.
o Assume that the target will travel at constant velocity during the prediction interval T. 
o Target’s future position is estimated by multiplying its velocity by T and adding that 
offset to its current position.
•
Evasion is the same idea, but applied to flee.

Boids Flocking: Neighbourhood
For boids flocking, the “neighbours” of the character are 
typically defined as all characters within a certain distance.
This can be made more realistic by assuming that the character 
has a limited field of view. Characters that are outside the field 
of view are not treated as neighbours.

Wander
•
Aim is to mimic realistic “casual” movement via random steering.
•
However, uniform random velocity changes don’t look realistic.
•
Better approach: Imagine a circle in front of the character and steer to a random point on the 
circle radius.
•
The circle size controls the amount of randomness.
•
See https://gamedevelopment.tutsplus.com/tutorials/understanding-steering-behaviors-
wander--gamedev-1624

Leader Following
•
Leader following can be used to mimic scenarios such a flock of sheep following a shepherd.
•
If a follower finds itself in a rectangular region in front of the leader, it will steer laterally away 
from the leader’s path.
•
Otherwise, the arrival target is a point offset slightly behind the leader.
•
The followers use separation behavior to prevent crowding.
•
See https://gamedevelopment.tutsplus.com/tutorials/understanding-steering-behaviors-
leader-following--gamedev-10810
31
Path following
•
Aim is to move a character along the path while staying within the specified radius of the spine.
•
If the projection distance is less than the path radius, no corrective steering is required.
•
Otherwise, seek towards the on-path projection of the predicted future position.
•
See https://gamedevelopment.tutsplus.com/tutorials/understanding-steering-behaviors-path-

Combining Steering Behaviors
•
Behaviours can be combined in two main ways:
o
Blending
o
Switching
•
Blending: Compute each of the steering forces 
separately then sum them together, possibly with 
different weighting factors. (This is how flocking works.)
o
Can be tricky, since the forces may cancel out or 
yield unintelligent behaviour when combined.
•
Switching: Toggle between different steering behaviours, 
depending on the current state of the world.
o
Next week we’ll look at finite state machines (FSMs), 
which can be used to determine when to switch 
between behaviours.
